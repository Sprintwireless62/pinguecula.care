Your care team

Who helps, and how to get culturally safe care

Eye care in Australia is a team effort. Here's who does what, how a referral works if you ever need one, what your optometrist can and can't do, and how to find eye care that's respectful and culturally safe.

Who's who in eye care

Several professionals may be involved, though for a straightforward pinguecula you'll usually only need an optometrist.[1,12]

Roles of the health professionals and services who may help with a pinguecula.
WhoHow they help
OptometristYour first point of contact. Examines and diagnoses the pinguecula, gives sun-protection advice, prescribes lubricating or (if therapeutically endorsed) anti-inflammatory drops, monitors it and refers on if needed.[12,13]
OphthalmologistA medical doctor and eye surgeon. Manages complex cases and performs surgery in the rare event a growth threatens vision.[3]
General practitioner (GP)Helps with overall health, related issues like allergies or dry eye, prescriptions, and coordinating referrals.[13]
PharmacistAdvises on choosing and correctly using over-the-counter lubricating drops.[1]
OrthoptistWorks alongside optometrists and ophthalmologists in eye clinics, assisting with eye assessments and vision testing.
Aboriginal Health Worker & ACCHOsAboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations and Aboriginal Health Workers provide culturally safe primary care and help coordinate access to eye services.[14,21]

What your optometrist can do

Optometrists are university-trained, registered eye care professionals and the first port of call for most eye concerns — in Australia they perform around 10 million eye checks each year.[13] For a pinguecula, an optometrist can fully diagnose it, explain it, advise on prevention, and manage the symptoms.

Many optometrists have therapeutic endorsement, which allows them to prescribe certain eye medicines — the anti-inflammatory drops most often used for a flare-up are among the most commonly prescribed.[12] This means you can often be diagnosed and treated in a single visit, without needing a separate appointment just for a prescription.[13]

An optometrist will refer you to an ophthalmologist if a growth begins to threaten vision or if surgery is being considered — a good example of optometrists and ophthalmologists working together.[12,13]

Urgent vs routine: knowing which pathway

Routine

Book a regular appointment

  • A new painless yellow spot you'd like checked
  • Mild, occasional dryness or grittiness
  • A pinguecula you already know about, for review
  • Choosing UV-protective sunglasses or lenses
Prompt / urgent

Be seen the same day

  • Sudden vision loss or significant pain
  • Rapid growth, bleeding or colour change
  • A growth reaching the clear centre of the eye
  • Eye injury, chemical splash or discharge

If you're not sure which applies, ring an optometry practice or your GP for advice. In an emergency call 000; in Victoria, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital has a 24-hour eye emergency department.

Culturally safe eye care

Acknowledgement of Country. We acknowledge the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Bunurong/Boon Wurrung peoples of the Kulin Nation as the Traditional Owners of the land on which our Melbourne practice stands, and pay respect to Elders past and present. We recognise the deep and continuing connection of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to Country, community and health.[22]

Everyone deserves eye care that is respectful, welcoming and free of judgement. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children often have better vision than other Australian children, yet as adults, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are about three times more likely to experience vision loss or blindness — and around 90% of that vision loss is preventable or treatable.[14,16] Much of this gap comes from barriers to care rather than from the eyes themselves.

Barriers can include distance from services, cost, past experiences of the health system, and language.[14,15] Good care works to remove these — for example by partnering with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations, offering interpreters where needed, and making the practice a place people feel safe to return to.[15,21] If you'd prefer an interpreter, a support person, or a longer appointment, just ask — that's a normal request.

Common questions

Is a pinguecula dangerous or cancerous?

No. It's a benign growth and does not spread inside the eye or elsewhere. If you're ever unsure whether a spot is a pinguecula, an optometrist can confirm it — occasionally a growth is examined more closely to be certain.[3,5]

Do I need surgery?

Almost never. Surgery is only considered for a pinguecula that's persistently inflamed, interferes with contact lenses, or bothers you cosmetically. Most people manage well with sun protection and drops.[1,3]

Can it come back after removal?

It can, particularly if UV exposure continues. Consistent sun protection afterwards makes a real difference to whether it returns.[3,9]

Is it caused by screen time?

No — screens don't emit meaningful UV. But we blink less at screens, and that dryness can make an existing pinguecula feel more irritated. Regular breaks and lubricating drops help.[1,5]

Do I need a GP referral to see an optometrist?

No. You can book an optometrist directly — they're the usual first point of contact for eye concerns and can refer you on if needed.[12,13]
