Who helps, and how to get culturally safe care

Eye care in Australia is a team effort. This page explains who does what, how a referral works if you ever need one, what your optometrist can and cannot do, and how to find eye care that is respectful and culturally safe.

Who is who in eye care

Several professionals may be involved, though for a straightforward pinguecula you will usually only need an optometrist.[21,22]

Roles of the health professionals and services who may help with a pinguecula.
WhoHow they help
OptometristYour first point of contact. Examines and diagnoses the pinguecula, gives sun-protection advice, prescribes lubricating or, if therapeutically endorsed, anti-inflammatory drops, monitors it, and refers on if needed.[21,22]
OphthalmologistA medical doctor and eye surgeon who manages complex cases and performs surgery in the rare event a growth threatens vision.[13]
General practitioner (GP)Helps with overall health, related issues such as allergies or dry eye, prescriptions, and coordinating referrals.[14]
PharmacistAdvises on choosing and correctly using over-the-counter lubricating drops.[1]
OrthoptistWorks alongside optometrists and ophthalmologists in eye clinics, assisting with eye assessments and vision testing.[29]
Aboriginal Health Worker and ACCHOsAboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations and Aboriginal Health Workers provide culturally safe primary care and help coordinate access to eye services.[24,26]

What your optometrist can do

Optometrists are university-trained, registered eye care professionals and the first port of call for most eye concerns.[22] For a pinguecula, an optometrist can fully diagnose it, explain it, advise on prevention, and manage the symptoms.

Many optometrists have therapeutic endorsement, which allows them to prescribe certain eye medicines, including the anti-inflammatory drops used for a flare-up.[21,22] This means you can often be diagnosed and treated in a single visit, without a separate appointment just for a prescription.[22]

An optometrist will refer you to an ophthalmologist if a growth begins to threaten vision or if surgery is being considered, which is a good example of optometrists and ophthalmologists working together.[13,21]

Urgent or routine: knowing which pathway

Routine

Book a regular appointment

  • A new painless yellow spot you would like checked[1]
  • Mild, occasional dryness or grittiness[1]
  • A pinguecula you already know about, for review[19]
  • Choosing UV-protective sunglasses or lenses[19]
Prompt or urgent

Be seen the same day

  • Sudden vision loss or significant pain[15,16]
  • Rapid growth, bleeding or colour change[17]
  • A growth reaching the clear centre of the eye[13]
  • Eye injury, chemical splash or discharge[14,16]

If you are not sure which applies, phone an optometry practice or your GP for advice. In an emergency call 000. In Victoria, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital has a 24-hour eye emergency department.[16]

Culturally safe eye care

Acknowledgement of Country. We acknowledge the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Bunurong peoples of the Kulin Nation as the Traditional Owners of the land on which this Melbourne practice stands, and pay respect to Elders past and present. We recognise the deep and continuing connection of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to Country, community and health.[28]

Everyone deserves eye care that is respectful, welcoming and free of judgement. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults are about three times more likely to experience vision loss or blindness than other Australians, and much of that vision loss is preventable or treatable.[23,24] A large part of the gap comes from barriers to care rather than from the eyes themselves.

Barriers can include distance from services, cost, past experiences of the health system, and language.[24,25] Good care works to remove these, for example by partnering with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations, offering interpreters where needed, and making the practice somewhere people feel safe to return to.[25,26] If you would prefer an interpreter, a support person, or a longer appointment, please ask, as that is a normal request.

Common questions

Is a pinguecula dangerous or cancerous?

No. It is a benign growth and does not spread inside the eye or elsewhere. If you are ever unsure whether a spot is a pinguecula, an optometrist can confirm it, and occasionally a growth is examined more closely to be certain, because rare surface cancers can look similar early on.[1,3,17]

Do I need surgery?

Almost never. Surgery is only considered for a pinguecula that is persistently inflamed, interferes with contact lenses, or bothers you cosmetically. Most people manage well with sun protection and drops.[1,13]

Can it come back after removal?

It can, particularly if UV exposure continues. Consistent sun protection afterwards makes a real difference to whether it returns.[6,13]

Is it caused by screen time?

No, screens do not emit meaningful UV. We do blink less at screens, though, and that dryness can make an existing pinguecula feel more irritated. Regular breaks and lubricating drops help.[1,6]

Do I need a GP referral to see an optometrist?

No. You can book an optometrist directly, as they are the usual first point of contact for eye concerns and can refer you on if needed.[21,22]
