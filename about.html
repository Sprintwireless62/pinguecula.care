Understanding pinguecula

A plain-language look at that yellow bump on the eye

Pinguecula (pin-GWEK-yoo-luh) is one of the most common growths on the surface of the eye. This page covers what it is, why it forms, how it differs from a look-alike condition, and what to do about it.

What it actually is

The conjunctiva is the thin, clear tissue covering the white of the eye. Over time, that tissue can develop a small, raised deposit made of protein, fat, and sometimes calcium. That deposit is a pinguecula — usually pale yellow, slightly domed, and sitting just beside the cornea rather than on it.

It's extremely common in adults, especially past age 40, and tends to grow slowly if at all. Vision is rarely affected, since it stays off the cornea, the clear window you actually see through.

Why it forms

The leading cause is cumulative ultraviolet exposure — the same kind of sun damage that ages skin. Wind and airborne dust add to the effect by drying and irritating the eye's surface over years of exposure.

  • Long hours outdoors without UV-blocking sunglasses
  • Living or working in dry, dusty, or windy climates
  • Chronic dry eye, which leaves the surface more exposed to irritation
  • Occupations with sun and wind exposure — fishing, farming, construction, lifeguarding

Signs to notice

Many pingueculae cause no symptoms at all and are only spotted during a routine eye exam. When symptoms do appear, they usually include:

  • A visible yellowish or pale bump on the white of the eye
  • Mild redness or a gritty, dry feeling
  • Occasional swelling or soreness — a flare-up sometimes called pingueculitis

If the bump becomes painful, changes shape quickly, or affects your sight, that's a reason to get it checked sooner rather than later.

Not the same as a pterygium

A pterygium shares the same causes but is a fold of tissue that actually grows across the cornea, which can blur vision as it advances. A pinguecula stays put beside the cornea and, left alone, can sometimes progress into a pterygium over many years — one more reason UV protection matters early on.

Prevention and everyday care

Block the UV

Wraparound sunglasses rated for 100% UVA/UVB, plus a brimmed hat, cut the exposure that starts and worsens a pinguecula.

Keep the surface moist

Preservative-free artificial tears ease the dryness and grit that often come with it, especially in wind or air conditioning.

Shield from dust and wind

Wraparound frames or goggles help in dusty, windy, or dry environments — cycling, farm work, or coastal jobs, for example.

When it flares up

A short course of anti-inflammatory drops, prescribed by an eye doctor, usually settles a red, sore episode within days.

Common questions

Does it ever need surgery?

Rarely. Surgical removal is only considered if it becomes chronically inflamed, causes significant irritation with contact lenses, or bothers someone for cosmetic reasons. Most people manage it with drops and sun protection alone.

Can it come back after removal?

It can, particularly if UV exposure continues unprotected. Sunglasses and hats after removal make a real difference to whether it returns.

Is it linked to screen time?

Not directly — screens don't emit meaningful UV. But reduced blinking during screen use can add to dryness, which makes an existing pinguecula feel more irritated.

Should I get it looked at?

If you've never had it examined, it's worth one visit to confirm what it is and rule out anything else. After that, most people just keep an eye on any change in size, colour, or comfort.
