The condition

Understanding your pinguecula

This page covers what a pinguecula is, why it forms, the symptoms it can cause, how it's likely to behave over time, and how to tell it apart from a related growth called a pterygium.

On this page: What it is Causes & risk factors Symptoms Likely course Pinguecula vs pterygium

What it actually is

The conjunctiva is the thin, clear tissue that covers the white of your eye. A pinguecula is a small, slightly raised deposit that builds up in this tissue — usually pale yellow, and sitting on the white of the eye just beside the cornea (the clear window at the front of the eye) rather than on it.[3,5]

Under the microscope it's a change in the eye's surface tissue, with deposits that can include protein, fat and sometimes calcium.[3,5] The name comes from the Latin pinguis, meaning "fat" — a nod to its yellowish, fatty appearance.[1] It most often appears on the side of the eye closest to the nose.[9]

Why it forms — causes and risk factors

The main cause is cumulative ultraviolet (UV) light — the same sun damage that ages skin. Over years, UV, together with wind and dust, changes the surface tissue of the eye and a pinguecula gradually forms.[1,3,9] Because the effect adds up over a lifetime, it becomes more common with age.[2,5]

Things that raise your risk:

  • Long-term sun exposure, especially without UV-blocking sunglasses[1,9]
  • Outdoor work or hobbies — the strongest everyday risk factor in research studies[1,8]
  • Dry, dusty, sandy or windy environments — common across much of Australia[1,4]
  • Older age — prevalence rises steadily through adulthood[2,5,7]
  • Chronic dry eye or eye irritation, which can make the surface more vulnerable[1,5]

Plain-language note: "UV" is the invisible part of sunlight that causes sunburn and, over time, this kind of change to the eye's surface. Reflected light — off the nose, water, sand or snow — adds to the dose, which is one reason a pinguecula usually appears on the nose side of the eye.[9]

Signs and symptoms

Many pingueculae cause no symptoms at all and are simply noticed in the mirror or found during a routine eye test.[3,5] When symptoms do occur, they're usually mild:

  • A visible yellowish or pale raised spot on the white of the eye[3]
  • A dry, gritty or "something-in-my-eye" feeling, because the bump can stop tears spreading evenly[1,5]
  • Occasional redness, swelling or soreness when it becomes inflamed — a flare-up called pingueculitis[1,2]
  • Sometimes brief blurring if the eye's surface dries out; the pinguecula itself doesn't sit over your line of sight[1]

Pingueculitis simply means the pinguecula has become irritated and inflamed. It's more uncomfortable and red than usual, and is often triggered by sun, wind, dust, very dry eyes or allergies.[2,4] It settles with the simple measures on the Managing it page.

How it's likely to behave over time

The outlook is reassuring. A pinguecula is benign (not cancer), grows slowly if at all, and for most people never affects vision.[1,3,5] It doesn't spread into the eye or to other parts of the body.[4]

In a small number of people, ongoing unprotected sun exposure can let it grow, and rarely it can progress into a pterygium — a related growth that can creep onto the cornea and, if it reaches far enough, blur vision.[2,4] This is one of the best reasons to protect your eyes from UV early (see Managing it).

The bottom line: most pingueculae are a harmless sign of past sun exposure. Sun protection and, if needed, lubricating drops keep them comfortable and slow any change.[1,3]

Pinguecula vs pterygium at a glance

They share the same causes and are easy to confuse. The key difference: a pinguecula stays on the white of the eye, while a pterygium can grow onto the cornea and affect sight.[3,4,5]
