Understanding your pinguecula

This page explains what a pinguecula is, why it forms, the symptoms it can cause, how it is likely to behave over time, and how to tell it apart from a related growth called a pterygium.

What it actually is

The conjunctiva is the thin, clear tissue that covers the white of the eye. A pinguecula is a small, slightly raised deposit that builds up in this tissue. It is usually pale yellow and sits on the white of the eye, just next to the cornea, which is the clear window at the front of the eye, rather than on it.[1,3]

Under the microscope it is a change in the surface tissue, where fibres and deposits collect after years of sun exposure.[1,12] The name comes from the Latin word for fat, a nod to its yellowish appearance.[1] It appears most often on the side of the eye closest to the nose.[2]

Why it forms: causes and risk factors

The main cause is cumulative ultraviolet (UV) light, the same sun damage that ages skin. Over many years, UV light, along with wind and dust, changes the surface tissue of the eye and a pinguecula gradually forms.[1,5,6] Because the effect adds up over a lifetime, it becomes more common with age.[8,9]

Factors that raise the risk include:

  • Long-term sun exposure, especially without UV-blocking sunglasses. Ocular sun exposure across the whole of life matters, not just recent years.[6]
  • Outdoor work or hobbies, which is one of the strongest everyday risk factors in population studies.[8,10]
  • Dry, dusty, sandy or windy environments, which are common across much of Australia.[1,4]
  • Older age, as prevalence rises steadily through adulthood.[7,8,9]
  • Chronic dryness or irritation of the eye surface.[1]

In plain terms: UV is the invisible part of sunlight that causes sunburn and, over time, this change to the eye surface. Light reflecting off the nose, water, sand and pavement adds to the dose, which is one reason a pinguecula usually appears on the side of the eye nearest the nose.[2,6]

Signs and symptoms

Many pingueculae cause no symptoms at all and are simply noticed in the mirror or found during a routine eye test.[1,3] When symptoms do occur, they are usually mild:

  • A visible yellowish or pale raised spot on the white of the eye.[3]
  • A dry or gritty feeling, as if something is in the eye, because the raised area can stop tears spreading evenly.[1]
  • Occasional redness, swelling or soreness when it becomes inflamed, a flare-up known as pingueculitis.[1,3]
  • Sometimes brief blurring if the surface dries out. The pinguecula itself does not sit over your line of sight.[1]

Pingueculitis simply means the pinguecula has become irritated and inflamed. It looks redder and feels more uncomfortable than usual, and is often triggered by sun, wind, dust, very dry eyes or allergies.[1,3] It settles with the simple measures on the Managing it page.

How it is likely to behave over time

The outlook is reassuring. A pinguecula is benign, meaning it is not cancer. It grows slowly if at all, and for most people it never affects vision.[1,3,13] It does not spread into the eye or to other parts of the body.[4]

In some people, continued unprotected sun exposure can let it grow, and it can progress into a pterygium, a related growth that can extend onto the cornea and, if it reaches far enough, blur vision.[3,4,5] This is one of the strongest reasons to protect the eyes from UV early, which is covered on the Managing it page.

The bottom line: most pingueculae are a harmless sign of past sun exposure. Sun protection and, if needed, lubricating drops keep them comfortable and slow any change.[1,3]

Pinguecula and pterygium compared

They share the same causes and are easy to confuse. The key difference is that a pinguecula stays on the white of the eye, while a pterygium can grow onto the cornea and affect sight.[3,4]
