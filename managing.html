Managing it

Keeping it comfortable, and living well with it

Most pingueculae need little more than sun protection and, if the eye feels dry, some lubricating drops. Here's how to care for yours, what treatments exist, how to protect your eyes, what to keep an eye on, and how it might affect everyday things like driving, work and contact lenses.

On this page: Self-care Treatments Prevention Everyday life Monitoring Red flags Care pathway

Everyday self-care

Block the UV

Wear close-fitting, wraparound sunglasses plus a broad-brimmed hat whenever you're outside in daylight. Together they can cut UV reaching the eyes by up to 98%.[10,11]

Keep the surface moist

Preservative-free artificial tears ease dryness and grittiness, especially in wind, air-conditioning or after screen time. They're available over the counter.[1,5]

Shield from wind & dust

Wraparound frames or safety eyewear help in dusty, sandy or windy settings — on the land, on the water, cycling or on a worksite.[1,3]

Don't rub

Rubbing adds irritation. If your eye feels gritty, a lubricating drop is kinder than rubbing and less likely to trigger a flare-up.[1]

Treatment options

Treatment is usually simple and matched to how much the pinguecula bothers you:

  • No symptoms? No treatment is needed — just sun protection and the occasional check.[1,3]
  • Dryness or grittiness? Lubricating (artificial tear) drops or ointment ease the discomfort.[1,5]
  • A red, inflamed flare-up (pingueculitis)? A short course of anti-inflammatory or mild steroid eye drops, prescribed by an optometrist or doctor, usually settles it within days.[1,3,5]
  • Rarely, surgery. Removal is only considered if the pinguecula is persistently inflamed, interferes with contact lens wear, or bothers you cosmetically — it isn't usually recommended.[1,3]

A word on eye drops: lubricating drops are safe to use often. Steroid or anti-inflammatory drops are prescription-only and used briefly and under supervision, because long-term use can have side effects — so use them only as directed.[5]

Preventing it getting worse

Because UV is the main driver, protecting your eyes is the single most useful thing you can do — it slows an existing pinguecula and lowers the chance of a new one or a pterygium.[9,11] In Australia, sunglasses are graded by the Australian/New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 1067; the swing tag shows a lens category from 0 to 4.[9,23]

Choosing sunglasses under the Australian/New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 1067. For sun protection, choose category 2, 3 or 4.[9,23]

0 – 1Fashion spectaclesLittle to no sun protection — not true sunglasses

  • Choose close-fitting, wraparound styles — they block light sneaking in from the sides.[9]
  • Add a broad-brimmed hat; hat plus sunglasses is far better than either alone.[11]
  • Price doesn't equal protection — look for the AS/NZS 1067 label, not a big brand.[9]
  • Wear eye protection year-round, not just in summer, and ask your optometrist about UV-protective prescription lenses.[9]

Everyday life: driving, work, contact lenses and vision

Driving

A pinguecula sits off to the side and doesn't block your line of sight, so it rarely affects driving.[1,5] If dryness is briefly blurring your vision, treat the dryness and have your eyes checked. Keep category-2 or 3 sunglasses in the car to cut glare.

Work, especially outdoors

If you work outdoors, your job is also your main risk factor.[1,8] Wear workplace eye protection that also blocks UV; occupational eyewear is covered by a separate standard (AS/NZS 1337). Employers can help supply suitable protection for sun, wind and dust.

Contact lenses

A raised pinguecula can occasionally make a contact lens feel less comfortable or affect how it sits.[1] If your lenses have become irritating, see your optometrist — a change of lens type or good lubrication often solves it.

Your vision and glasses

A pinguecula doesn't usually change your glasses prescription. Keep up your regular eye examinations so any change is picked up early, and your optometrist can prescribe UV-protective lenses if you wear glasses outdoors.[1,9]

Monitoring — what to keep an eye on

Once an optometrist has confirmed it's a pinguecula, you mostly just watch for change:

  • Any change in size, colour or shape, or a growth starting to reach the cornea[2,4]
  • Flare-ups that keep coming back, or new discomfort[2]
  • Attend your routine eye examinations — a good moment to review it and your sun protection[9]
Don't wait

Red-flag symptoms

These are not typical of a pinguecula and need prompt assessment by an optometrist, GP or eye emergency department:

  • • Sudden loss or blurring of vision
  • • Significant or worsening eye pain
  • • A spot that grows quickly, bleeds or changes colour
  • • A growth creeping onto the clear centre of the eye
  • • New light sensitivity or discharge
  • • An eye injury or chemical splash

In an emergency call 000. In Victoria, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital runs a 24-hour eye emergency department.

A typical care pathway

Most people follow a simple path. An optometrist is usually the first and main point of contact.[12,13]

  1. You notice a spot

    A yellowish bump on the white of the eye, or mild dryness and grittiness.

  2. Optometrist examination

    A painless slit-lamp check confirms it's a pinguecula and rules out other lumps.[5]

  3. Advice and self-care

    Sun-protection advice and lubricating drops if needed; drops for any flare-up.[1,3]

  4. Monitor and review

    Watch for change and review at your regular eye tests.[2,9]

  5. Referral only if needed

    If it threatens vision or needs surgery, your optometrist refers you to an ophthalmologist.[3,12]
