Keeping it comfortable, and living well with it

Most pingueculae need little more than sun protection and, if the eye feels dry, some lubricating drops. This page covers how to care for yours, the treatments that exist, how to protect your eyes, what to watch for, and how it might affect everyday activities such as driving, work and contact lenses.

On this page: Self-care Treatments Prevention Everyday life Monitoring Red flags Care pathway

Everyday self-care

Block the UV

Wear close-fitting, wraparound sunglasses and a broad-brimmed hat whenever you are outside in daylight. Ocular protection is beneficial at every age, so it is worth doing consistently.[6,18]

Keep the surface moist

Preservative-free artificial tears ease dryness and grittiness, especially in wind, air-conditioning or after screen time. They are available over the counter.[1,13]

Shield from wind and dust

Wraparound frames or safety eyewear help in dusty, sandy or windy settings, on the land, on the water, cycling or on a worksite.[1,3]

Try not to rub

Rubbing adds irritation. If the eye feels gritty, a lubricating drop is gentler than rubbing and less likely to trigger a flare-up.[1]

Treatment options

Treatment is usually simple and matched to how much the pinguecula bothers you:

  • No symptoms. No treatment is needed, just sun protection and the occasional check.[1,3]
  • Dryness or grittiness. Lubricating (artificial tear) drops or ointment ease the discomfort.[1,13]
  • A red, inflamed flare-up. A short course of anti-inflammatory or mild steroid eye drops, prescribed by an optometrist or doctor, usually settles pingueculitis within days.[1,13]
  • Rarely, surgery. Removal is only considered if the pinguecula is persistently inflamed, interferes with contact lens wear, or bothers you cosmetically. It is not usually recommended.[1,13]

A note on eye drops: lubricating drops are safe to use often. Steroid or anti-inflammatory drops are prescription-only and are used briefly and under supervision, because longer-term use can have side effects. Use them only as directed.[13]

Preventing it from getting worse

Because UV is the main driver, protecting your eyes is the single most useful thing you can do. It slows an existing pinguecula and lowers the chance of a new one, or of a pterygium. Protection helps at every age.[6,18] In Australia, sunglasses are graded under the Australian and New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 1067, and the swing tag shows a lens category from 0 to 4.[19,20]

Choosing sunglasses under AS/NZS 1067. For sun protection, choose category 2, 3 or 4.[19,20]
Lens categoryWhat it is forSun and UV protection
0 to 1Fashion spectaclesLittle to no sun protection, not true sunglasses
2General sunglassesGood UV protection, medium glare reduction

  • Choose close-fitting, wraparound styles, which block light coming in from the sides.[19]
  • Add a broad-brimmed hat. A hat with sunglasses protects far better than either alone.[18]
  • Price does not equal protection. Look for the AS/NZS 1067 label rather than a brand name.[19,20]
  • Wear eye protection year-round, not only in summer, and ask your optometrist about UV-protective prescription lenses.[19]

Everyday life: driving, work, contact lenses and vision

Driving

A pinguecula sits off to the side and does not block your line of sight, so it rarely affects driving.[1] If dryness is briefly blurring your vision, treat the dryness and have your eyes checked. Keep category 2 or 3 sunglasses in the car to reduce glare.[19]

Work, especially outdoors

If you work outdoors, your job is also your main risk factor.[8,10] Wear workplace eye protection that also blocks UV, and where sun, wind or dust are part of the job, ask your employer about suitable protective eyewear.[6,19]

Contact lenses

A raised pinguecula can sometimes make a contact lens feel less comfortable or affect how it sits.[1] If your lenses have become irritating, see your optometrist, as a change of lens type or good lubrication often solves it.[1,13]

Your vision and glasses

A pinguecula does not usually change your glasses prescription. Keep up your regular eye examinations so any change is picked up early, and your optometrist can prescribe UV-protective lenses if you wear glasses outdoors.[1,19]

Monitoring: what to keep an eye on

Once an optometrist has confirmed it is a pinguecula, you mostly watch for change:

  • Any change in size, colour or shape, or a growth starting to reach the cornea.[13,17]
  • Flare-ups that keep returning, or new discomfort.[1]
  • Attend your routine eye examinations, which are a good moment to review it and your sun protection.[19]

Any spot that grows quickly, bleeds or changes colour should be checked promptly, because rare surface growths can look similar in their early stages.[17]

Do not wait

Red-flag symptoms

These are not typical of a pinguecula and need prompt assessment by an optometrist, GP or eye emergency department:

  • • Sudden loss or blurring of vision[16]
  • • Significant or worsening eye pain[15]
  • • A spot that grows quickly, bleeds or changes colour[17]
  • • A growth reaching the clear centre of the eye[13]
  • • New light sensitivity or discharge[14]
  • • An eye injury or a chemical splash[16]

In an emergency call 000. In Victoria, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital runs a 24-hour eye emergency department.[16]

A typical care pathway

Most people follow a simple path, and an optometrist is usually the first and main point of contact.[21,22]

  1. You notice a spot

    A yellowish bump on the white of the eye, or mild dryness and grittiness.[1,3]

  2. Optometrist examination

    A painless slit-lamp check confirms it is a pinguecula and rules out other lesions.[1,17]

  3. Advice and self-care

    Sun-protection advice and lubricating drops if needed, plus drops for any flare-up.[1,3]

  4. Monitor and review

    Watch for change and review it at your regular eye tests.[17,19]

  5. Referral only if needed

    If it threatens vision or needs surgery, your optometrist refers you to an ophthalmologist.[13,21]
