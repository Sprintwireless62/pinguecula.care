Plain-language patient guide

That yellow spot on the white of your eye, explained clearly

A pinguecula (said pin-GWEK-you-la) is a small, harmless bump on the white of the eye. It is a sign of long-term sun exposure, and in a sunny climate like Victoria's, many adults develop one, particularly people who have spent years working or spending time outdoors.[1,2,6]

Understand the condition When to seek help

Written for patients and their families. This is general information and does not replace a personal eye examination.[1]

Pinguecula: a small, yellow patch or bump that forms on the conjunctiva, the thin, clear membrane covering the sclera (the white of the eye).[1,3]

Why this site exists

This site was written by Tasha Tahir, an optometry student, to help you recognise a pinguecula, understand why it forms, and feel confident about what to do next. It covers simple self-care, the warning signs that mean you should be seen promptly, and the health professionals who can help. Everything is written in everyday language and is supported by clinical guidelines and peer-reviewed research.[1,2]

Seek care promptly

When a spot on your eye needs urgent attention

A pinguecula itself is harmless. The symptoms below are different and should not wait, because each can point to a more serious eye problem. Contact an optometrist, your GP, or an eye emergency department the same day if you have:

  • • Sudden loss or blurring of vision[16]
  • • Significant or worsening eye pain, rather than mild grittiness[15]
  • • A spot that grows quickly, bleeds, or changes colour[17]
  • • A growth spreading onto the clear cornea at the centre of the eye[13]
  • • New light sensitivity, discharge, or a red and painful eye[14]
  • • Any eye injury or a chemical splash to the eye[16]

In an emergency call 000. For urgent eye problems in Victoria, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital has a 24-hour emergency department.[16]

How common is it?

48%

of adults aged 40 and over had a pinguecula in a large general-population study[8]

~89%

of people who have one have it in both eyes[8]

2×

higher odds of having one with regular outdoor activity[8]

Pinguecula becomes more common with age because it reflects a lifetime of sun exposure. It is more frequent in people who work or play outdoors, such as farmers, tradespeople, gardeners, fishers and surfers, and in hot, dry, dusty or windy conditions like much of Australia.[1,7,8]

Reassuring to know

A pinguecula is not cancer and does not spread inside the eye. For most people it never affects sight and needs no treatment beyond sun protection and, if the eye feels dry, some lubricating drops.[1,2,13] Understanding what it is often takes the worry away.
